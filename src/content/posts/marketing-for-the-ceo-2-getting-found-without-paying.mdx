## Free costs time, not money

In [Chapter 1](../marketing-for-the-ceo-1-the-marketing-map/) you met owned, earned and paid attention. This chapter is the owned and earned side: channels where you don't pay per person who sees you.

"Free" is a little misleading. You pay in time and patience instead of cash. Somebody has to write the pages, polish the store listing, show up in communities and send the emails. With an AI team doing most of that work, time is cheaper for you than for most small studios. That makes these channels a good place to start.

<aside class="callout analogy">
<p class="callout-title">In the shop</p>
<p>Picture your shop again. You can get customers without buying a single flyer:</p>
<ul>
<li>Be on a <strong>busy street</strong>, so people walk past it anyway (search and the app store).</li>
<li>Dress the <strong>shop window</strong> so passers-by step inside (your store page).</li>
<li><strong>Chat at the local club</strong> where your kind of customer hangs out (communities).</li>
<li>Throw a <strong>grand-opening day</strong> (launch sites).</li>
<li>Hand out a <strong>loyalty card</strong> and let <strong>regulars bring friends</strong> (email list and referrals).</li>
</ul>
</aside>

## Search: answer the question people type

<dfn>SEO</dfn> (search engine optimization) means making your web pages show up when people search on Google and other search engines. It is how your shop ends up on the busy street.

It starts with <dfn>keywords</dfn>: the actual words people type. Behind every search is a <dfn>search intent</dfn>, the thing the person really wants. "who wins a tie in Catan" means "settle this argument at my table, now". It does not mean "sell me an app".

So the job is simple to say: for each question your users ask, have one page on your site that answers it clearly, better than anything else out there. For RuleBuddy that could be one short page per popular game and common rules question, each ending with a gentle "ask RuleBuddy about any game".

<aside class="callout watch">
<p class="callout-title">Watch out</p>
<p>SEO is slow. New pages often take weeks to months before they show up well, so judge it after a quarter, not a week. And never pad pages with keywords or mass-produce thin copies: search engines treat that as spam and can push the whole site down.</p>
</aside>

## Getting found in AI answers

More and more people skip the list of links. They ask ChatGPT, Gemini or Claude, or read the AI summary Google shows at the top of the results (<dfn>AI Overviews</dfn>, and the chat-style <dfn>AI Mode</dfn>). The assistant reads the web for them and names a few apps or pages.

So there's a new goal next to SEO: be one of the sources the AI *cites*, the pages and products it names in its answer. People call this <dfn>AEO</dfn> (answer engine optimization) or <dfn>GEO</dfn>; it's the same idea.

<aside class="callout analogy">
<p class="callout-title">In the shop</p>
<p>Picture a well-known local guide standing on the street. Shoppers ask "where can I get X?" and the guide names two or three shops. The guide recommends shops they've read clear things about, and shops other people keep mentioning.</p>
</aside>

What gets you named, as far as anyone can tell in 2026:

- **Clear pages that answer real questions.** The same rules-answer pages from the last section. A plain question as the heading and a direct answer in the first lines are easy for an assistant to quote.
- **Being mentioned elsewhere.** Assistants lean on what others say: Reddit threads, reviews, YouTube videos, "best apps for…" comparison pages. Honest mentions in those places count for more than anything on your own site.
- **Being specific and trustworthy.** The web is filling with bland AI-written pages. Real details (actual rulings from actual games, who wrote it, when it was checked) stand out, to people and to the AI that picks sources.

<aside class="callout note">
<p class="callout-title">As of 2026</p>
<p>Google says its AI Overviews and AI Mode each reach well over a billion people a month. Each assistant cites different sources, and which sites they favour shifts from month to month. Ranking well on Google helps with Google's AI answers, but it predicts much less for ChatGPT or Gemini. Nobody can promise a citation, so treat this as part of SEO, not a separate bet.</p>
</aside>

<aside class="callout watch">
<p class="callout-title">Watch out</p>
<p>Don't flood the web with AI-written pages or fake "reviews" to get mentioned. It's easy to spot, communities ban it, and it's the exact noise the assistants try to filter out. A few real, specific pages beat a hundred generic ones.</p>
</aside>

<p class="ask"><strong>Tell the team:</strong> "Ask ChatGPT, Gemini and Google which app helps with board game rules. Tell me who gets named and why, and what we'd need for RuleBuddy to be on that list."</p>

## The app store: your shop window

<dfn>ASO</dfn> (app store optimization) is SEO for the app store. People search inside Google Play and Apple's App Store too, and your listing is the shop window they judge you by. RuleBuddy is on Google Play today, with iPhone planned, so this matters now.

Four things do most of the work:

- **Name and subtitle.** Say what it is in the words people search: "board game rules" beats a clever slogan.
- **Keywords.** The search words the store matches you on. On Apple you list them in a hidden field; Google Play picks them up from your description.
- **Screenshots.** Most people look at the first two or three and decide. Each one should show one benefit with a short caption, not a bare settings screen.
- **Ratings and reviews.** Proof that other people liked it. They help you rank and help people say yes.

<aside class="callout note">
<p class="callout-title">As of 2026</p>
<p><strong>Apple App Store:</strong> name up to 30 characters, subtitle up to 30, plus a hidden keyword field of 100 characters. <strong>Google Play:</strong> title up to 30 characters, short description up to 80, full description up to 4,000, and no keyword field, so the words in your text matter more. Both stores give apps an official in-app "rate this app" prompt and limit how often it can appear.</p>
</aside>

<aside class="callout watch">
<p class="callout-title">Watch out</p>
<p>Never buy reviews, swap fake ones, or offer rewards for five stars. Both stores ban it and can pull the app. The honest version works: ask for a rating right after a good moment, like a question answered well.</p>
</aside>

## Social and communities: be a regular, not a billboard

<dfn>Content</dfn> is anything useful or fun you publish: short videos, posts, guides. On social media (TikTok, Instagram, YouTube Shorts, X, and so on) it can reach people who weren't searching for you at all. A 30-second "the rule everyone gets wrong in Ticket to Ride" clip fits RuleBuddy well. One idea can become a page, a video and a post, so plan ideas, not posts.

<dfn>Communities</dfn> are places where your kind of person already gathers: subreddits on Reddit, forums like BoardGameGeek, Discord servers, Facebook groups. Board gamers are a very community-minded crowd, which helps you.

The rule in communities is the rule at the local club: people welcome a regular who helps, and they show a salesman the door. Answer questions, be useful, mention the app only where it truly answers the question, and say you made it.

RuleBuddy has a head start: it already has a Discord bot. A board game Discord server that adds the bot puts RuleBuddy in front of every member, every game night. Offer it to server admins; don't push it on them.

<aside class="callout watch">
<p class="callout-title">Watch out</p>
<p>Many subreddits and groups ban or limit self-promotion, and each has its own rules. Read them first. A burst of copy-paste posts from a new account gets removed, and can get the app's name banned in exactly the place you most want to be.</p>
</aside>

<p class="ask"><strong>Tell the team:</strong> "List the ten biggest board game communities, their self-promotion rules, and a plan to be genuinely helpful in three of them."</p>

## Launch days, the email list and referrals

### Launch sites: one loud day

<dfn>Launch sites</dfn> such as Product Hunt (and, for technical products, "Show HN" on Hacker News) are where people go to see new products, ranked by votes that day. A good launch is your grand-opening day: a burst of visitors, some press-like attention, a few useful backlinks. Then it goes quiet. It's a spike, not a stream.

### The email list: your loyalty card

An <dfn>email list</dfn> is the people who gave you their address so you can write to them. It is the most *owned* channel you have: no algorithm decides whether they see you, and no platform can take it away. Use it for real news (new games supported, the iPhone version) and the occasional useful tip, not daily nagging. Collect addresses from day one, even before you need them, like a waitlist for the iPhone app.

### Referrals: regulars bringing friends

<dfn>Word of mouth</dfn> is users telling others on their own. A <dfn>referral program</dfn> nudges it along: give users an easy way to invite a friend, often with a small reward for both. RuleBuddy has a built-in advantage here, since board games are played in groups: one user at the table can win over three more.

## Where to start

You can't do all of these well at once. Here is how they compare. The timings are rough rules of thumb, not promises.

A sensible order for one app: fix the shop window (ASO) so every visitor counts, then start the slow builders (search pages and community presence), and keep the email list and referrals running in the background. Save the launch day for something worth announcing.

<aside class="callout why">
<p class="callout-title">Why it matters</p>
<p>Ads stop the day you stop paying. Free channels keep working: a good answer page, a strong store listing and a happy community bring people in next year too. They also make paid ads cheaper later, because a better shop window turns more ad clicks into installs. <a href="../marketing-for-the-ceo-3-paying-for-attention/">Chapter 3</a> covers the paid side.</p>
</aside>

<li>Fix the shop window first (name, screenshots, ratings), then build the slow channels that compound: pages that answer real questions and being a helpful regular in communities.</li>
<li>Own your audience with an email list and let happy users bring friends; never fake reviews or spam groups.</li>
