## What marketing is for

If you run a small product or business, you already know how to make the thing people buy. Marketing is everything that happens so the *right people* find it, try it, keep using it, and tell others about it.

That's the whole job. Ads, search, social posts and emails are just different ways of doing one of those four things. When something sounds confusing later, ask: "which of the four is this helping with?"

To keep things concrete, this series follows one example all the way through: Pantry Pal, a made-up meal-planning app for busy families. It isn't a real product, so swap in whatever you sell.

"The right people" matters. A thousand random downloads that are deleted the same day are worth less than fifty parents who open Pantry Pal every Sunday to plan the week.

<aside class="callout analogy">
<p class="callout-title">In the shop</p>
<p>Picture your app as a <strong>small shop</strong>. The <strong>sign out front</strong> tells passers-by what you sell. Being on a <strong>busy street</strong> brings walk-ins for free. The <strong>shop window</strong> is the first look that makes people step inside. <strong>Flyers and billboards</strong> cost money and bring people who'd never have walked past. And <strong>regulars</strong> who tell their friends are the cheapest customers you'll ever get.</p>
<p>We'll come back to this shop all through the book.</p>
</aside>

## The funnel: find, try, keep, tell

Every user goes through the same four steps. Marketers draw them as a <dfn>funnel</dfn>: wide at the top because many people see you, narrow at the bottom because few stay.

1. **Find.** Someone sees Pantry Pal: a search result, a post, an ad, a friend's phone.
2. **Try.** They visit the store page and install it, or open the website.
3. **Keep (and pay).** They come back the next week to plan again. If there's a paid tier, this is where they pay.
4. **Tell others.** They share the week's plan with a partner or a friend. Those friends enter at the top, for free.

The share of people who move from one step to the next is a <dfn>conversion rate</dfn>. If 100 people see the store page and 20 install, that step converts at 20%. Keeping users over time is called <dfn>retention</dfn>.

<aside class="callout why">
<p class="callout-title">Why it matters</p>
<p>The funnel tells you <strong>where to spend effort</strong>. If people install and then leave, more ads just buy more people who leave. Fix the leakiest step first. Often that's the store page or the first five minutes in the app, not "we need more traffic".</p>
</aside>

<p class="ask"><strong>Tell the team:</strong> "Show me our funnel, with the number at each step, and tell me which step leaks most."</p>

## Positioning: the sign out front

<dfn>Positioning</dfn> is deciding, in plain words, *who the app is for* and *the one thing it promises them*. It comes before any ad or post, because every ad and post repeats it.

A handy fill-in-the-blanks line: "For *\[who\]* who *\[problem\]*, *\[app\]* *\[does what\]*."

<div class="compare">
<div>
<p class="compare-label bad">Vague sign</p>
<p>"Pantry Pal: an AI-powered meal-planning platform."</p>
<p>Who's it for? What does it do for me tonight? People walk past.</p>
</div>
<div>
<p class="compare-label good">Clear sign</p>
<p>"For busy parents who dread 'what's for dinner?', Pantry Pal plans the week's meals and the shopping list in five minutes."</p>
<p>A tired parent reads it and thinks "that's me".</p>
</div>
</div>

(For your own product, picking the real line is one of the first marketing jobs, whoever ends up doing it.)

Good positioning is narrow on purpose. "Everyone who eats" is too wide to write an ad for. "Parents of young kids who cook most nights and hate the 5 p.m. scramble" is something an ad, a Reddit post and an app store screenshot can all speak to.

<aside class="callout watch">
<p class="callout-title">Watch out</p>
<p>The most common mistake is describing <strong>how it works</strong> ("AI-powered", "built with…") instead of <strong>what it does for the person</strong>. Users don't care about the engine. They care that dinner is sorted.</p>
</aside>

<p class="ask"><strong>Tell the team:</strong> "Give me three one-line promises for our product, each aimed at a different kind of customer, and say which you'd test first."</p>

## Three kinds of attention: owned, earned, paid

Every way of reaching people is called a <dfn>channel</dfn>: Google search, the App Store, Instagram, Reddit, email, Facebook ads. There are dozens, but they fall into three buckets.

- <dfn>Owned</dfn>: places you control. Your website, your app store page, your email list, your social accounts. It costs time, not money, and it's yours forever.
- <dfn>Earned</dfn>: attention other people give you. Reviews, word of mouth, a Reddit thread recommending you, a blog that writes you up. Free and very trusted, but you can't switch it on.
- <dfn>Paid</dfn>: attention you rent. Ads on Facebook, Instagram, Google, the App Store, TikTok. Fast and controllable, but it stops the moment you stop paying.

The three feed each other. A good store page (owned) makes every ad (paid) convert better. Happy users leave reviews (earned) that make the store page better. An email list (owned) lets you ask happy users for a review.

<aside class="callout note">
<p class="callout-title">Not every app needs this</p>
<p>Marketing is for products you want strangers to find. An app you built for your own family, or a tool only your staff use, needs none of this. This series is about the kind you want the world to find.</p>
</aside>

## A shop vs an app: what changes for software

The shop is a good picture, but an app isn't a bakery. Five differences change how you market software:

<div class="table-wrap">
<table>
<thead>
<tr><th>A shop</th><th>An app</th></tr>
</thead>
<tbody>
<tr><td>Every loaf costs flour and time.</td><td><strong>Extra copies cost nothing.</strong> The 10,000th user costs about the same as the 10th, so growth is cheap once something works.</td></tr>
<tr><td>You taste a sample, then buy.</td><td><strong>Try before you pay.</strong> A <dfn>free trial</dfn> or <dfn>freemium</dfn> (free basic version, paid extras) lets people use the real thing first.</td></tr>
<tr><td>Your street is a few blocks.</td><td><strong>The whole world is the street,</strong> and the street is the app store, Google and, more and more, AI assistants (<a href="../marketing-for-the-ceo-2-getting-found-without-paying/#getting-found-in-ai-answers">Chapter 2</a>).</td></tr>
<tr><td>A sale is a sale.</td><td><strong>Subscriptions mean keeping users matters as much as getting them.</strong> A user who cancels after a month barely pays back what it took to win them.</td></tr>
<tr><td>Word of mouth happens by luck.</td><td><strong>The product can market itself:</strong> share links, invites, "shared from Pantry Pal" moments, referral rewards.</td></tr>
</tbody>
</table>
</div>

That last one has a name: <dfn>product-led growth</dfn>, growth where using the app is itself what brings in the next user. When a parent sends the week's shopping list to their partner, the product just did a bit of marketing.

<p class="ask"><strong>Tell the team:</strong> "Where in our product could a happy user naturally share something with a friend, and what would make that one tap?"</p>

## Your job as CEO

You don't need to write ads or pick keywords yourself. A marketer, an agency or a set of AI tools can do that. Your job is the same as everywhere else in the business: set the goal, approve the big calls, and read the results.

- **Decide who it's for** and approve the one-line promise.
- **Pick the step to fix** this month, from the funnel numbers.
- **Set the budget**, in time and money, and when to stop a test.

Chapter 2 covers the free roads into the shop, Chapter 3 the paid ones, and [Chapter 4](../marketing-for-the-ceo-4-running-it-as-ceo/) is the cheat sheet for running it all.
