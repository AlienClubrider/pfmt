The marketing map. Part 1 of 4 of Marketing for the CEO.

Marketing looks like a hundred unrelated things: ads, SEO, posts, emails, reviews. It is really one simple path with a few roads leading into it. This chapter gives you that map, so every later idea has a place to sit.

# What marketing is for

You already know how to build a good app. Marketing is everything that happens so the right people find it, try it, keep using it, and tell others about it.

That's the whole job. Ads, search, social posts and emails are just different ways of doing one of those four things. When something sounds confusing later, ask: "which of the four is this helping with?"

"The right people" matters. A thousand random downloads that are deleted the same day are worth less than fifty board gamers who open RuleBuddy every game night.

In the shop: Picture your app as a small shop. The sign out front tells passers-by what you sell. Being on a busy street brings walk-ins for free. The shop window is the first look that makes people step inside. Flyers and billboards cost money and bring people who'd never have walked past. And regulars who tell their friends are the cheapest customers you'll ever get.

We'll come back to this shop all through the book.

Figure: Each part of a shop matched to its marketing name: sign is positioning, busy street is search and app store, window is the store page, flyers are ads, regulars are retention, friends told are referrals.

# The funnel: find, try, keep, tell

Every user goes through the same four steps. Marketers draw them as a funnel: wide at the top because many people see you, narrow at the bottom because few stay.

1. Find. Someone sees RuleBuddy: a search result, a post, an ad, a friend's phone.

2. Try. They visit the store page and install it, or open the website.

3. Keep (and pay). They come back the next game night. If there's a paid tier, this is where they pay.

4. Tell others. They show it to their gaming group. Those friends enter at the top, for free.

The share of people who move from one step to the next is a conversion rate. If 100 people see the store page and 20 install, that step converts at 20%. Keeping users over time is called retention.

Figure: A funnel with made-up numbers: 1,000 people see the app, 150 open the store page, 30 install, 8 still use it after a month, 2 tell a friend.

Why it matters: The funnel tells you where to spend effort. If people install and then leave, more ads just buy more people who leave. Fix the leakiest step first. Often that's the store page or the first five minutes in the app, not "we need more traffic".

Tell the team: "Show me our funnel for RuleBuddy, with the number at each step, and tell me which step leaks most."

# Positioning: the sign out front

Positioning is deciding, in plain words, who the app is for and the one thing it promises them. It comes before any ad or post, because every ad and post repeats it.

A handy fill-in-the-blanks line: "For [who] who [problem], [app] [does what]."

Vague sign.

"RuleBuddy: an AI-powered board game companion app."

Who's it for? What does it do for me tonight? People walk past.

Clear sign.

"For board gamers who argue about the rules, RuleBuddy answers any rules question in seconds."

A gamer reads it and thinks "that's me".

(That second line is an example to show the idea, not RuleBuddy's agreed tagline. Picking the real one is one of the first jobs for your strategist.)

Good positioning is narrow on purpose. "Everyone who plays games" is too wide to write an ad for. "Groups who play heavy board games and lose 10 minutes to rulebook lookups" is something an ad, a Reddit post and an app store screenshot can all speak to.

Watch out: The most common mistake is describing how it works ("AI-powered", "built with...") instead of what it does for the person. Users don't care about the engine. They care that the argument at the table ends.

Tell the team: "Give me three one-line promises for RuleBuddy, each aimed at a different kind of player, and say which you'd test first."

# Three kinds of attention: owned, earned, paid

Every way of reaching people is called a channel: Google search, the App Store, Instagram, Reddit, email, Facebook ads. There are dozens, but they fall into three buckets.

Owned: places you control. Your website, your app store page, your email list, your social accounts. It costs time, not money, and it's yours forever.

Earned: attention other people give you. Reviews, word of mouth, a Reddit thread recommending you, a blog that writes you up. Free and very trusted, but you can't switch it on.

Paid: attention you rent. Ads on Facebook, Instagram, Google, the App Store, TikTok. Fast and controllable, but it stops the moment you stop paying.

Figure: Three columns. Owned: website, store page, email list, social accounts; costs time. Earned: reviews, word of mouth, posts, press; free but out of your control. Paid: Facebook, Google, App Store and TikTok ads; costs money and stops when you stop.

The three feed each other. A good store page (owned) makes every ad (paid) convert better. Happy users leave reviews (earned) that make the store page better. An email list (owned) lets you ask happy users for a review.

Not every app needs this: Marketing is for apps you want strangers to find. The family apps are private, so they get no marketing at all. RuleBuddy is the one this book is about.

# Your job as CEO

You don't need to write ads or pick keywords. Your AI marketing team will. Your job is the same as on the dev side: set the goal, approve the big calls, and read the results.

Decide who it's for and approve the one-line promise.

Pick the step to fix this month, from the funnel numbers.

Set the budget, in time and money, and when to stop a test.

Chapter 2 covers the free roads into the shop, Chapter 3 the paid ones, and Chapter 4 is the cheat sheet for running it all.

Recap:

1. Marketing gets the right people to find, try, keep and tell others about the app. Every tactic serves one of those four steps.

2. Positioning (who it's for, one clear promise) comes first. Every ad, post and screenshot repeats it.

3. Attention is owned (yours), earned (given) or paid (rented). Fix the leakiest funnel step before paying for more people at the top.
