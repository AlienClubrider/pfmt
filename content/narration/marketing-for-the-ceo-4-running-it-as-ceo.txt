# The 5 numbers to watch

In the shop: A shop owner doesn't read every receipt. They glance at a few things each week: how many people walked past, how many came in, how many bought, how many came back, and what the flyers cost. That's your whole dashboard too.

Ask the analyst for these five, once a week, for each app you market. They follow the funnel from top to bottom.

1. Reach. How many people saw the app or its store page this week. Is the top of the funnel growing?

2. Store conversion. Of the people who saw the store page, what share installed. This tells you if the shop window works.

3. Retention. Of the people who installed, what share still use it 7 days later and 30 days later (day-7 and day-30 retention). This tells you if the app keeps its promise.

4. CAC (cost to get one user). Total marketing money spent divided by new users. Only matters once you pay for ads.

5. Revenue per user, and over time LTV (what one user is worth before they leave). Compare it with CAC: see the money math.

Figure: A weekly dashboard with five tiles and made-up example values: reach 4,000, store conversion 18 percent, day-30 retention 12 percent, CAC 2 dollars 50, revenue per user 1 dollar 80.

Why it matters: These five tell you which step to fix next, which is the one decision only you make. Everything else (likes, followers, "impressions") is detail. If someone reports a big number that isn't one of these five, ask how it moved one of them.

Tell the team: "Every Monday, send me the five numbers for RuleBuddy with last week next to them, and one sentence on what changed."

# Setting a budget

A marketing budget is two things: time (your team's hours on free channels) and money (ads, tools). For a small app, spend time first. Free channels from Chapter 2 build things you keep: a store page, pages that rank on Google, an email list.

Money comes in once the funnel holds water. Then treat ad spend as a series of small, capped tests, never one big bet.

Figure: A made-up 300 dollar test budget is split into three 100 dollar ad tests. Two lose and are stopped; the winner gets the remaining money.

Rule of thumb: Early on, think roughly most effort on free channels, a small capped amount on paid tests. Raise the paid share only when payback is clear: a user earns back what they cost within a few months.

Watch out: Always give the team a hard cap (per day and per month) and a stop rule ("pause any ad that costs more than $X per install after $Y spent"). Ad platforms will happily spend whatever you allow, and they suggest raising budgets often.

# Your AI marketing team

On the dev side you have agents that write, review and ship code. The marketing side works the same way: a few roles, each with a clear job, and a strategist who coordinates. I'll set them up and run them; you talk to the strategist and read the analyst's numbers.

AI changed the work more than the roles. Making ten ad versions or a month of post drafts is now cheap, and ad platforms target automatically. What stays scarce is judgement: a sharp promise, real and specific content, and knowing when to stop spending. That's where your OK matters.

Watch out: Anything that goes out in public or spends money needs your OK: posting under your name, replying in communities, launching ads, emailing users. Agents draft; you approve. Communities in particular spot and ban copy-paste promotion fast.

# A first 30-day plan for RuleBuddy

Five steps, roughly a week each. It starts free and ends with one small paid test, so you learn the whole funnel before money is at stake.

1. Write the sign. The strategist proposes who RuleBuddy is for and the one-line promise. You pick one. (Positioning.)

2. Measure the funnel. The analyst checks what's already tracked (store page views, installs, retention) and fills the gaps, then reports the five numbers as a starting point.

3. Fix the shop window. Rewrite the store title, subtitle, keywords and screenshots around the promise, and add one or two website pages that answer real rules questions. Check what ChatGPT, Gemini and Google's AI answer say when asked about board game rules apps. (App store, search.)

4. Show up where gamers are. Be genuinely helpful in a few board game communities, start an email list on the website, and ask happy users for a rating. (Communities.)

5. Run one small paid test, then review. A capped ad test with a stop rule, aimed at the audience in your promise. At day 30, look at the five numbers and choose next month's one focus. (Test small.)

# Glossary

Funnel: The steps every user goes through: find, try, keep (and pay), tell others. Wide at the top, narrow at the bottom.

Conversion rate: The share of people who move from one step to the next, for example, store page visitors who install.

Retention (day-7, day-30): The share of new users still using the app 7 or 30 days after installing.

Positioning: Who the app is for and the one thing it promises them. The sign out front.

Channel: Any way of reaching people: Google, the App Store, Reddit, email, Facebook ads.

Owned or earned or paid: Attention you control (site, store page, email list), attention others give you (reviews, word of mouth, press), attention you rent (ads).

Organic: Users who came without an ad: search, the store, word of mouth.

Freemium or free trial: A free basic version with paid extras or full access free for a short time. Both let people try before they pay.

Product-led growth: Growth where using the app brings in the next user: share links, invites, referrals.

SEO: Search engine optimization: making your web pages show up when people search Google.

Keyword: The words people type into a search box.

Search intent: What the searcher actually wants behind the words they typed.

AI answers (AEO or GEO): Being one of the sources ChatGPT, Gemini, Claude or Google's AI Overviews and AI Mode name when people ask a question. The AI-era add-on to SEO.

ASO: App store optimization: SEO for the App Store and Google Play (title, keywords, screenshots, ratings).

Content: Anything you publish to be useful or interesting: articles, posts, short videos.

Email list: People who gave you their email to hear from you. The loyalty card; owned, so no platform can take it away.

Referral: A user bringing in a friend; a referral program makes it easy and sometimes rewards both.

Campaign: One ad effort with its own goal, audience, ads and budget.

Creative: The ad itself: image or video, and the words. With automated targeting, the main thing you control.

Automated campaigns: Ad products where the platform picks the audience and placement for you, for example, Meta Advantage+ and Google App campaigns (as of 2026; names change).

Impressions: How many times an ad was shown.

CTR: Click-through rate: the share of people who saw an ad and tapped it.

CPM or CPC or CPI: Cost per 1,000 impressions or per click or per install.

CAC: Customer acquisition cost: what you spend, on average, to get one new user or customer.

LTV: Lifetime value: how much money one user brings in before they leave.

Payback: How long it takes a user's payments to earn back their CAC.

Retargeting: Showing ads to people who already visited but didn't install or buy. The reminder flyer.

Attribution: Working out which channel or ad a user came from. Never perfectly accurate, especially on iPhone since Apple's 2021 privacy changes.

Recap:

1. Watch five numbers weekly: reach, store conversion, retention, CAC and revenue per user. Fix the step that leaks most.

2. Spend time on free channels first; spend money in small capped tests with a stop rule, and give more only to what earns it back.

3. You direct the strategist and read the analyst. Nothing public goes out and no money is spent without your OK.
