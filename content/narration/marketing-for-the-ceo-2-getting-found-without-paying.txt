Getting found without paying. Part 2 of 4 of Marketing for the CEO.

Most of an app's best users find it without a single ad: they search, browse the app store, hear about it in a group they trust, or get sent a link by a friend. This chapter walks through each of those free channels, what it's for, how long it takes, and what you'd ask the team to do.

# Free costs time, not money

In Chapter 1 you met owned, earned and paid attention. This chapter is the owned and earned side: channels where you don't pay per person who sees you.

"Free" is a little misleading. You pay in time and patience instead of cash. Somebody has to write the pages, polish the store listing, show up in communities and send the emails. AI tools can now draft a lot of that work, so the time cost is lower than it used to be. That makes these channels a good place to start.

In the shop: Picture your shop again. You can get customers without buying a single flyer:

Be on a busy street, so people walk past it anyway (search and the app store).

Dress the shop window so passers-by step inside (your store page).

Chat at the local club where your kind of customer hangs out (communities).

Throw a grand-opening day (launch sites).

Hand out a loyalty card and let regulars bring friends (email list and referrals).

# Search: answer the question people type

SEO (search engine optimization) means making your web pages show up when people search on Google and other search engines. It is how your shop ends up on the busy street.

It starts with keywords: the actual words people type. Behind every search is a search intent, the thing the person really wants. "chicken and rice dinner ideas" means "give me something to cook tonight". It does not mean "sell me an app".

So the job is simple to say: for each question your users ask, have one page on your site that answers it clearly, better than anything else out there. For Pantry Pal that could be one short page per common dinner question, each ending with a gentle "plan your whole week with Pantry Pal".

Figure: A person searches a dinner question, picks the result that matches their question, reads the answer, and a few go on to try the app while most leave with their answer.

Google and chat assistants now often answer before anyone clicks. The next section covers that.

Watch out: SEO is slow. New pages often take weeks to months before they show up well, so judge it after a quarter, not a week. And never pad pages with keywords or mass-produce thin copies: search engines treat that as spam and can push the whole site down.

Tell the team: "Find the 20 questions our customers search most, and write one honest answer page for each."

# Getting found in AI answers

More and more people skip the list of links. They ask ChatGPT, Gemini or Claude, or read the AI summary Google shows at the top of the results (AI Overviews, and the chat-style AI Mode). The assistant reads the web for them and names a few apps or pages.

So there's a new goal next to SEO: be one of the sources the AI cites, the pages and products it names in its answer. People call this AEO (answer engine optimization) or GEO; it's the same idea.

In the shop: Picture a well-known local guide standing on the street. Shoppers ask "where can I get X?" and the guide names two or three shops. The guide recommends shops they've read clear things about, and shops other people keep mentioning.

What gets you named, as far as anyone can tell in 2026:

Clear pages that answer real questions. The same answer pages from the last section. A plain question as the heading and a direct answer in the first lines are easy for an assistant to quote.

Being mentioned elsewhere. Assistants lean on what others say: Reddit threads, reviews, YouTube videos, "best apps for..." comparison pages. Honest mentions in those places count for more than anything on your own site.

Being specific and trustworthy. The web is filling with bland AI-written pages. Real details (recipes actually cooked and timed, who wrote it, when it was checked) stand out, to people and to the AI that picks sources.

As of 2026: Google says its AI Overviews and AI Mode each reach well over a billion people a month. Each assistant cites different sources, and which sites they favour shifts from month to month. Ranking well on Google helps with Google's AI answers, but it predicts much less for ChatGPT or Gemini. Nobody can promise a citation, so treat this as part of SEO, not a separate bet.

Watch out: Don't flood the web with AI-written pages or fake "reviews" to get mentioned. It's easy to spot, communities ban it, and it's the exact noise the assistants try to filter out. A few real, specific pages beat a hundred generic ones.

Tell the team: "Ask ChatGPT, Gemini and Google which product they'd recommend for the problem we solve. Tell me who gets named and why, and what we'd need to be on that list."

# The app store: your shop window

ASO (app store optimization) is SEO for the app store. People search inside Google Play and Apple's App Store too, and your listing is the shop window they judge you by. If your product is an app, this matters from day one.

Four things do most of the work:

Name and subtitle. Say what it is in the words people search: "meal planner" beats a clever slogan.

Keywords. The search words the store matches you on. On Apple you list them in a hidden field; Google Play picks them up from your description.

Screenshots. Most people look at the first two or three and decide. Each one should show one benefit with a short caption, not a bare settings screen.

Ratings and reviews. Proof that other people liked it. They help you rank and help people say yes.

Figure: An app store listing on a phone, with labels for the name and subtitle, ratings, screenshots, description and the hidden keyword field.

As of 2026: Apple App Store: name up to 30 characters, subtitle up to 30, plus a hidden keyword field of 100 characters. Google Play: title up to 30 characters, short description up to 80, full description up to 4,000, and no keyword field, so the words in your text matter more. Both stores give apps an official in-app "rate this app" prompt and limit how often it can appear.

Watch out: Never buy reviews, swap fake ones, or offer rewards for five stars. Both stores ban it and can pull the app. The honest version works: ask for a rating right after a good moment, like a week planned in one go.

Tell the team: "Rewrite our store listing around the words people search, redo the first three screenshots, and add a rating prompt after a good moment."

# Social and communities: be a regular, not a billboard

Content is anything useful or fun you publish: short videos, posts, guides. On social media (TikTok, Instagram, YouTube Shorts, X, and so on) it can reach people who weren't searching for you at all. A 30-second "five dinners from one grocery run" clip fits Pantry Pal well. One idea can become a page, a video and a post, so plan ideas, not posts.

Communities are places where your kind of person already gathers: subreddits on Reddit, forums, group chats, Facebook groups. Parents swap meal ideas all the time, which helps you.

The rule in communities is the rule at the local club: people welcome a regular who helps, and they show a salesman the door. Answer questions, be useful, mention the app only where it truly answers the question, and say you made it.

Look for ways the product itself can live where people gather. If Pantry Pal let a parent post the week's plan straight into a family group chat, everyone in that chat would see it. Offer things like that where they help; don't push them.

Watch out: Many subreddits and groups ban or limit self-promotion, and each has its own rules. Read them first. A burst of copy-paste posts from a new account gets removed, and can get the app's name banned in exactly the place you most want to be.

Tell the team: "List the ten biggest communities where our customers gather, their self-promotion rules, and a plan to be genuinely helpful in three of them."

# Launch days, the email list and referrals

# Launch sites: one loud day

Launch sites such as Product Hunt (and, for technical products, "Show HN" on Hacker News) are where people go to see new products, ranked by votes that day. A good launch is your grand-opening day: a burst of visitors, some press-like attention, a few useful backlinks. Then it goes quiet. It's a spike, not a stream.

Figure: A sketch of visitors per week over twelve weeks. A launch day spikes and then falls back to almost nothing. Search and communities start near zero and climb steadily.

# The email list: your loyalty card

An email list is the people who gave you their address so you can write to them. It is the most owned channel you have: no algorithm decides whether they see you, and no platform can take it away. Use it for real news (a big new feature, a version for a new platform) and the occasional useful tip, not daily nagging. Collect addresses from day one, even before you need them, like a waitlist for a version you haven't built yet.

# Referrals: regulars bringing friends

Word of mouth is users telling others on their own. A referral program nudges it along: give users an easy way to invite a friend, often with a small reward for both. Pantry Pal has a built-in advantage here, since meal plans get shared: one parent who uses it can win over a partner and a couple of friends.

Figure: A referral loop: a happy user shares an invite link, a friend considers it and joins, both get a reward, and the friend invites the next person.

Tell the team: "Start an email list now with a waitlist for our next big feature, and design a simple invite-a-friend reward."

# Where to start

You can't do all of these well at once. Here is how they compare. The timings are rough rules of thumb, not promises.

The table rates each free channel for Pantry Pal: the app store and search are high and come first, communities and referrals are high, social content and launch sites are medium, and an email list should start now, small.

A sensible order for one app: fix the shop window (ASO) so every visitor counts, then start the slow builders (search pages and community presence), and keep the email list and referrals running in the background. Save the launch day for something worth announcing.

Why it matters: Ads stop the day you stop paying. Free channels keep working: a good answer page, a strong store listing and a happy community bring people in next year too. They also make paid ads cheaper later, because a better shop window turns more ad clicks into installs. Chapter 3 covers the paid side.

Recap:

1. Free channels cost time instead of money: search pages, the store listing, communities, content, launch days, email and referrals.

2. Fix the shop window first (name, screenshots, ratings), then build the slow channels that compound: pages that answer real questions and being a helpful regular in communities.

3. Own your audience with an email list and let happy users bring friends; never fake reviews or spam groups.
